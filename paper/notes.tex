\begin{abstract}
  Scrap document recording the state of the discussion on calibrating local
  stochastic volatility models by neural $L^2$ minimisation in place of the
  kernel-based particle method. Two schemes are recorded, an explicit
  per-time-slice scheme and an implicit global scheme with damped fixed-point
  iteration, together with a sketch of the convergence analysis for the latter
  and the practical considerations identified so far. Nothing here is final.
\end{abstract}

\section{Model and calibration condition}\label{sec:setup}

We consider a local stochastic volatility (LSV) model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lsv}
  \frac{dX_t}{X_t} = \Lev(t, X_t)\,\sqrt{V_t}\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $V$ is a stochastic variance factor (Heston or similar) driven by a
Brownian motion $B$ with $d\langle W, B\rangle_t = \rho\,dt$, and
$\Lev\colon [0,T]\times\R_+ \to \R_+$ is the leverage function.

By Gy\"ongy's mimicking theorem, the Markovian projection of \cref{eq:lsv} has
local variance $\Lev^2(t,x)\,\E[V_t \mid X_t = x]$. The model reprices all
vanilla options if and only if this equals the Dupire local variance, which
forces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leverage}
  \Lev(t,x) = \frac{\sigDup(t,x)}{\sqrt{\E[V_t \mid X_t = x]}}.
\end{equation}
The conditional expectation in \cref{eq:leverage} is taken under the law
generated by $\Lev$ itself, so the calibrated SDE is nonlinear in the sense of
McKean. Define the self-consistency map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phimap}
  \Phimap\colon \Lev \longmapsto
  \frac{\sigDup}{\sqrt{\E^{\Lev}[V_t \mid X_t = \cdot\,]}},
\end{equation}
where $\E^{\Lev}$ denotes expectation under the law of the solution of
\cref{eq:lsv} with leverage $\Lev$. A calibrated model is a fixed point of
$\Phimap$.

\section{Conditional expectation by $L^2$ minimisation}\label{sec:l2}

The methodological core is the projection identity: over all measurable
functions $f$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l2proj}
  \E[V_t \mid X_t = \cdot\,]
  = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{f}\; \E\bigl[(V_t - f(X_t))^2\bigr].
\end{equation}
Given a simulated particle cloud $\{(X^{(i)}_t, V^{(i)}_t)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, we
train a neural network $f_\theta$ by minimising the empirical squared loss and
read off the conditional expectation. No kernel, no bandwidth selection, no
density estimation. This replaces the Nadaraya--Watson step of the
Guyon--Henry-Labord\`ere particle method.

\section{Explicit scheme (per time slice)}\label{sec:explicit}

On a time grid $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_K = T$, use one network per slice.
At $t_k$, train $f_{\theta_k}(x) \approx \E[V_{t_k} \mid X_{t_k} = x]$ on the
particle cloud at $t_k$ only, set
\[
  \Lev(t_k, x) = \frac{\sigDup(t_k, x)}{\sqrt{f_{\theta_k}(x)}},
\]
and advance the particles to $t_{k+1}$. The network takes only $x$ as input.

Structural observation to state early in any write-up: the explicit scheme
contains \emph{no fixed point}. Since $\Lev(t_k,\cdot)$ depends only on
$\Lev(t_j,\cdot)$ for $j < k$, the discretised problem is a causal forward
recursion solved in a single pass by construction. The existence question of
\cref{sec:theory} is therefore not load-bearing for the explicit scheme; it
bites only for the implicit one.

\section{Implicit scheme (global network, damped iteration)}\label{sec:implicit}

A single network handles all times,
\[
  f_\theta(t, x) \approx \E[V_t \mid X_t = x],
\]
trained on the pooled data $\{(t_k, X^{(i)}_{t_k}, V^{(i)}_{t_k})\}_{i,k}$.
The leverage is updated by the damped fixed-point iteration
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damped}
  \Lev^{n+1} = (1 - \alpha)\,\Lev^{n} + \alpha\,\Phimap(\Lev^{n}),
  \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1].
\end{equation}

The scheme is implicit for a subtle reason: shared parameters $\theta$ let data
at times $t_j > t_k$ influence $\Lev(t_k, \cdot)$, so information flows
backward through $\theta$. That, rather than the time stepping, is the actual
implicitness. A practical advantage is that sharing across $t$ borrows strength
into data-poor slices (tails, short maturities).

\section{Convergence analysis sketch for the implicit scheme}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Damping algebra}

Write $\Phal \coloneqq (1-\alpha)\Id + \alpha\Phimap$ and linearise at a fixed
point $\Lev^*$. Each spectral value $\lambda$ of $D\Phimap(\Lev^*)$ is mapped to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mumap}
  \mu = (1-\alpha) + \alpha\lambda = 1 + \alpha(\lambda - 1).
\end{equation}
One has $\abs{\mu} < 1$ for some $\alpha > 0$ if and only if
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 1$, with the per-eigenvalue threshold
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold}
  \alpha < \frac{2\,(1 - \operatorname{Re}\lambda)}{\abs{1 - \lambda}^2},
\end{equation}
and the overall threshold is the infimum of \cref{eq:threshold} over the
spectrum. Two consequences worth recording.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Damping cannot rescue any spectral value with
    $\operatorname{Re}\lambda \geq 1$. For real $\lambda > 1$,
    $\mu = 1 + \alpha(\lambda - 1) > 1$ for every $\alpha > 0$. The structural
    hypothesis below is therefore the real content of the convergence claim.
  \item Damping never changes the fixed-point set,
    $\Phal(\Lev) = \Lev \iff \Phimap(\Lev) = \Lev$. Damping buys convergence,
    never existence. Existence must come from a separate argument.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{assumption}[Spectral condition]\label{ass:spectral}
  The spectrum of $D\Phimap(\Lev^*)$ is contained in
  $\{z \in \mathbb{C} : \operatorname{Re} z < 1\}$.
\end{assumption}

Heuristic support for \cref{ass:spectral}: the oscillatory overshoot observed
in undamped iterations of the classical calibration loop suggests spectral
values with negative real part, which is exactly the regime where damping helps
most. \inlinetodo{Explore estimating the dominant eigenvalue numerically, e.g.\
power iteration on the calibration loop; would turn the assumption into a
measurement.}

\subsection{Existence versus local convergence: two separate routes}

The following two arguments must not be conflated.

\emph{Ostrowski (spectral radius) route.} Assuming a fixed point $\Lev^*$
exists and $\Phimap$ is Fr\'echet differentiable at $\Lev^*$, the condition
$\rho\bigl(D\Phal(\Lev^*)\bigr) < 1$ yields local linear convergence of
\cref{eq:damped} from a sufficiently good initial guess, via the Gelfand
equivalent-norm construction. In function space one must speak of the spectrum
rather than eigenvalues. This is a local convergence theorem and gives no
existence.

\emph{Banach route.} Existence and uniqueness require $\Phal$ to be a
contraction on a complete metric space that it maps into itself. At the linear
level in a Hilbert norm the sharp condition concerns the \emph{numerical range}
of $D\Phimap$, which contains the spectrum and is in general strictly larger:
one needs the numerical range in $\{\operatorname{Re} z < 1\}$ together with a
bound on $\norm{D\Phimap}$. Stronger hypothesis, global conclusion (existence,
uniqueness, geometric convergence).

\emph{Honest fallback structure.} Establish existence by a short-horizon
contraction argument (Lipschitz constant of $\Phimap$ of order $CT$, matching
the short-time results in the literature), or by Schauder compactness for the
discretised problem (existence without uniqueness); then apply the Ostrowski
route for local convergence of the damped iteration.

\subsection{Fr\'echet differentiability of $\Phimap$: the hard step}

The map $\Phimap$ factors as
$\Lev \mapsto \text{law of } (X, V) \mapsto \E[V_t \mid X_t = \cdot\,]
\mapsto \sigDup / \sqrt{\cdot}$, and with joint density $p$,
\[
  \E[V_t \mid X_t = x] = \frac{\int v\, p(x, v)\, dv}{\int p(x, v)\, dv},
\]
so differentiating $\Phimap$ in $\Lev$ means differentiating a ratio of
densities with respect to an SDE coefficient. Required ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item existence and smoothness of the joint density of $(X_t, V_t)$;
  \item a strictly positive lower bound on the $X$-marginal on the region
    where $\Lev$ is evaluated;
  \item differentiability of $\Lev \mapsto p^{\Lev}$, by Malliavin calculus or
    parabolic PDE perturbation theory.
\end{enumerate}
The denominator bound in item 2 is the tail-degeneracy issue from practice
reappearing in the theory. Capping or truncating the spatial domain is not an
engineering hack; it is what makes $\Phimap$ differentiable on a manageable
set. This alignment deserves a remark in the paper.

\subsection{The implementable theorem}

The algorithm iterates a perturbed map $\widehat{\Phimap} = \Phimap +
\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ collects the neural regression error and the
finite-particle error. Target statement: under \cref{ass:spectral} and for
$\alpha$ below the threshold \cref{eq:threshold}, the damped iteration on
$\widehat{\Phimap}$ converges to an $\varepsilon/(1-r)$-neighbourhood of
$\Lev^*$, where $r < 1$ is the contraction factor of the linearised damped map.
This has the same structure as the deep BSDE error analyses, and it is the
statement the experiments can verify.

\subsection{State of the well-posedness literature}

Well-posedness of the calibrated McKean--Vlasov equation is open in full
generality. Milestones: short-time existence for the associated nonlinear PIDE
(Abergel--Tachet 2010); existence for finite-state volatility (Jourdain--Zhou);
inversion of the Markovian projection and stationary solutions under
restrictions (Lacker--Shkolnikov--Zhang 2019); existence under non-regular
coefficients with propagation of chaos (Djete 2022); strong
existence-uniqueness for finite-state volatility with propagation of chaos
(Mustapha 2024). Continuous-state Heston-type LSV remains unresolved without
regularisation. Any fixed-point claim should therefore be made for the
discretised or regularised map, or under \cref{ass:spectral} as a named
hypothesis, and not in a generality exceeding these results. See
\texttt{papers/READING\_LIST.md} for the annotated list.

\section{Practical considerations}\label{sec:practical}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Positivity.} Nothing constrains $f_\theta \geq 0$ and
    \cref{eq:leverage} divides by $\sqrt{f_\theta}$. Since $V \geq 0$ implies
    $\E[V \mid X] \geq 0$, parametrise $f_\theta = \operatorname{softplus}(g_\theta)$
    or learn $\log \E[V \mid X]$; costs nothing in expressiveness and encodes a
    true property.
  \item \emph{Tail weighting.} The $L^2$ loss is weighted by the sampling
    density of $X_t$, so errors in the tails are barely penalised, precisely
    where out-of-the-money leverage lives. The classical particle method papers
    over this with capping. The principal remedy is the Girsanov importance
    sampling of \cref{sec:girsanov}; the cross-time strength borrowing of the
    implicit scheme also helps.
  \item \emph{Dupire input.} $\sigDup$ requires an arbitrage-free implied
    volatility surface; treat as given and cite the standard machinery.
  \item \emph{Time interpolation (explicit scheme).} $\Lev$ is defined on grid
    times; intermediate times need interpolation, as in the classical particle
    method.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Importance sampling by change of measure}\label{sec:girsanov}

As the particles spread, few of them populate the wings, and the regression
estimate of $\E[V_t \mid X_t = x]$ degrades exactly where out-of-the-money
leverage is needed. The kernel method suffers there too. The remedy is a
Girsanov change of measure with an explicit density process, used as
importance sampling.

\subsection{A two-dimensional change that moves only the stock}

Write the volatility driver as $B = \rho W + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\, W^\perp$ with
$(W, W^\perp)$ independent Brownian motions, and assume $\abs{\rho} < 1$.
Perform a two-dimensional Girsanov change with kernel $(\theta, \theta^\perp)$
subject to the constraint
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constraint}
  \rho\,\theta + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\theta^\perp = 0 .
\end{equation}
Under the new measure $Q$, the process $B$ receives drift
$\rho\theta + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\theta^\perp = 0$ and therefore remains a
Brownian motion, so the dynamics of $V$ are unchanged, and with the same
realisation of $B$ the $V$ paths are literally identical. Meanwhile $W$
acquires drift $\theta$, so the stock particles are pushed into the wings.
This leaves a one-parameter family indexed by $\theta$, which may be
deterministic in $t$ or state dependent. The density process is the explicit
stochastic exponential accumulated along the simulation.

\subsection{Weighted $L^2$ regression recovers the $P$-conditional expectation}

Let $Z = dP/dQ$. For any measurable $f$, the weighted loss
$\E_Q\!\left[ Z\,(V_t - f(X_t))^2 \right]$ is minimised pointwise by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weightedreg}
  f(x) = \frac{\E_Q[Z V_t \mid X_t = x]}{\E_Q[Z \mid X_t = x]}
       = \E_P[V_t \mid X_t = x],
\end{equation}
by the abstract Bayes formula. Importance-weighted $L^2$ minimisation
therefore recovers the correct conditional expectation with no further
correction: the tilt moves where the samples live, the weights fix the
estimand. The identical weighting applies to Nadaraya--Watson regression, so
importance sampling is orthogonal to the neural-versus-kernel comparison and
improves both.

\begin{remark}[Self-normalisation]\label{rem:selfnorm}
  Importance sampling for conditional expectations is more favourable than
  for unconditional ones. The ratio structure of \cref{eq:weightedreg}
  self-normalises at each $x$, so the level of $Z$ at $x$ cancels; only the
  conditional variability of $Z$ given $X_t = x$ contributes noise. That
  variability comes from the volatility path and vanishes in the
  deterministic-volatility limit. The effective sample size per spatial
  bucket is the natural diagnostic for tuning $\theta$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Exact discrete-time weights]\label{rem:discrete}
  In the simulated chain no continuous-time Girsanov argument is needed:
  draw the tilted Gaussian increments under $Q$ and accumulate the exact
  ratio of Gaussian densities step by step. The resulting weights are exact
  for the discretised chain, with no discretisation error in the measure
  change itself.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Defensive mixtures]\label{rem:mixture}
  A single tilt direction covers one wing. Splitting the cloud into
  subpopulations with tilts $+\theta$, $-\theta$ and $0$, combined with
  balance-heuristic mixture weights, covers both wings while avoiding weight
  blow-up.
\end{remark}

\section{Warm starting and update scheduling}\label{sec:warmstart}

\subsection{Across time slices in the explicit scheme}

The target function $\E[V_{t_k} \mid X_{t_k} = \cdot\,]$ moves by $O(\Delta t)$
between adjacent slices, so initialising $\theta_k$ at $\theta_{k-1}$ leaves
only a few gradient steps of work per slice. This is a large constant-factor
reduction in training cost.

\subsection{Reduced update frequency at later times}

Early in the simulation the cloud spreads from a point mass and the
correlation structure between $X$ and $V$ builds quickly, so the conditional
expectation surface changes fast; later, mean reversion of $V$ drives it
toward quasi-stationarity and updates can be less frequent, improving
latency. A caution: a stale $f$ biases $\Lev$ at the skipped times, and this
error feeds forward through the SDE and compounds, which cuts against the
exact-calibration selling point. Prefer an event-triggered refresh: monitor
the regression loss of the current network on the fresh cloud and retrain
when it crosses a threshold. This yields a latency-accuracy trade-off with a
defensible rule, and a natural trade-off plot for the experiments.

\subsection{Explicit output as initialiser for the implicit scheme}

Run the explicit pass first and use it to initialise the implicit one:
distil the per-slice networks into the global network $f_\theta(t,x)$ as
supervised pretraining, and take $\Lev^{0} = \Lev_{\mathrm{explicit}}$. This
is more than engineering. The Ostrowski route of \cref{sec:theory} delivers
only local convergence from a sufficiently good initial guess, and the
explicit pass constructively supplies one: its output differs from the fixed
point only by time-discretisation bias and regression noise. The explicit
scheme is thus the initialiser that plausibly places the damped iteration
inside its convergence basin. The engineering trick and the hypothesis of the
local convergence theorem are the same statement.

\section{Positioning}\label{sec:positioning}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Versus Guyon--Henry-Labord\`ere.} Replace kernel regression with
    neural $L^2$ regression: no bandwidth selection, and (to be demonstrated,
    not asserted) better bias-variance behaviour in data-poor regions.
  \item \emph{Versus Cuchiero--Khosrawi--Teichmann.} They parametrise $\Lev$
    directly and calibrate to prices; we derive $\Lev$ from Gy\"ongy so vanilla
    calibration holds by construction modulo regression error. Make the
    contrast explicit.
  \item \emph{Versus Bayer et al.\ 2022 (RKHS).} Closest prior work: a rigorous
    version of smoothing the conditional-expectation estimator to make the
    fixed-point map tractable. Our one-line positioning is to replace their
    RKHS ridge regression with neural $L^2$ regression. Read before writing the
    differentiability section.
  \item \emph{Versus Reisinger--Tsianni.} Discretisation-error analysis of the
    classical particle scheme; the natural baseline for the
    $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood statement.
  \item \emph{The high bar.} In one dimension the particle method is fast and
    works well. Kernel regression genuinely fails, and networks do not, in
    multi-asset LSV. If there is a dimensional story, that is the strongest
    version of the paper.
\end{itemize}
